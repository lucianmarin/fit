# High-Fiber and High-Spermidine Foods

Reference tables of foods notably high in dietary fiber and in the polyamine **spermidine**, compiled from USDA FoodData Central, peer-reviewed polyamine-content studies, and the [spermidinefoods.com](https://www.spermidinefoods.com/database/) database.

> **Note:** Values are approximate. Fiber content varies with variety, ripeness, and preparation (raw vs. cooked). Spermidine content varies even more — by cultivar, fermentation, aging, and storage — so figures below are drawn from multiple independent analyses and should be read as representative ranges, not fixed constants.

## What Is the "High-Fiber Spermidine Diet"?

It isn't one diet — it's two diets layered together:

1. **A high-fiber diet**, built around legumes, bran, seeds, whole grains, and vegetables.
2. **A high-spermidine diet**, built around foods like wheat germ, aged cheese, soy, mushrooms, and legumes.

Each has its own, separately studied benefits — fiber for digestion, satiety, cholesterol, and gut microbiome diversity; spermidine for autophagy and the cardiovascular/mortality associations seen in the Bruneck cohort. What makes combining them worth calling out is a mechanistic link between the two: dietary fiber isn't just fuel for your own digestion, it's also fuel for the gut bacteria (notably *Bacteroides* and *Fusobacterium* species) that synthesize spermidine and putrescine in your colon. Studies feeding fermentable fiber (e.g., pectin) to animals have shown measurably higher cecal spermidine output as a result. In other words, eating more fiber can help your gut *produce* more spermidine, on top of whatever spermidine you eat directly — so the two intakes plausibly reinforce each other rather than just adding up side by side.

It's also worth being clear-eyed about the evidence: the diet-microbiota-polyamine link is well established mechanistically and in animal models, and the spermidine-mortality association in humans comes from large observational cohorts (Bruneck, SAPHIR) rather than randomized trials proving cause and effect. Treat this as a well-motivated eating pattern, not a guaranteed medical intervention.

## Recreating It in Your Own Kitchen

Neither of these two tables was designed by anyone — legumes, whole grains, mushrooms, and vegetables just happen to have evolved to be both fibrous *and* polyamine-rich, because fiber (structural plant carbohydrate) and spermidine (a compound involved in cell division and tissue growth) both concentrate in the same actively-growing parts of a plant: seeds, germ, and sprouting tissue. That overlap at the food-category level — legumes, whole grains, cruciferous vegetables — is what makes it practical to build meals that lean on both tables at once, rather than eating fiber and spermidine as two separate chores.

A few pairing ideas to combine a High-Fiber food with a High-Spermidine food in one dish:

- **Lentils or split peas + mushrooms** — a lentil-mushroom soup or stew
- **Oats + wheat germ** — stir a spoonful of wheat germ into morning oatmeal
- **Black beans or navy beans + aged cheese** — a bean dish finished with shaved parmesan or aged cheddar
- **Chia pudding + mango** — layer chia pudding with fresh mango
- **Whole-grain toast + natto or tempeh** — a savory, umami-rich breakfast
- **Roasted broccoli or cauliflower + almonds** — toss with toasted almonds for crunch

Because both tables span grains, legumes, vegetables, fruits, nuts, and dairy, there's enough variety here to rotate through very different cuisines and flavor profiles — Japanese (natto, miso, brown rice), Mediterranean (chickpeas, olive oil, aged cheese), or simple home cooking (oats, beans, wheat germ) — without repeating the same few dishes.
